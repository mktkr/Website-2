## Arizona Doesn't Wait for Your Family to Figure It Out

When someone passes away without a will or trust, the legal term is "dying intestate." It doesn't mean your family gets nothing. It means Arizona's legislature — not you — already decided who gets what.

Arizona's intestate succession laws are found in Title 14, Chapter 2 of the Arizona Revised Statutes. They lay out a rigid hierarchy of who inherits, in what order, and in what proportions. The rules apply automatically. There is no negotiation, no flexibility, and no consideration for your actual relationships or intentions.

For some families, the default rules happen to line up with what they would have chosen anyway. For many others, especially blended families, unmarried couples, and people with complicated relationships, the results are nothing close.

## Arizona's Default Rules: Who Gets What

Arizona is a community property state, which means most assets acquired during a marriage belong equally to both spouses. That matters here because the intestacy rules treat community property and separate property differently.

### If you're married with children — and they're all shared children

Your surviving spouse inherits your entire estate. That includes your half of the community property and all of your separate property. This is the simplest scenario, and the one that lines up with what most people would have wanted. (A.R.S. § 14-2102(1).)

### If you're married with children from a different relationship

This is where the surprises start. Your surviving spouse receives only half of your separate property and *none* of your half of the community property. Your children — including children from a prior relationship — inherit the rest by representation. (A.R.S. § 14-2102(2).)

<div class="callout-bronze">
  <p>Read that again. In a blended family, Arizona law can send your half of the community property — the house, the savings accounts, the retirement you built during this marriage — directly to your children and away from the spouse you're currently married to.</p>
</div>

### If you're not married

Your children inherit everything, divided equally, by representation. If a child of yours has already passed away, that child's share passes down to their own children — your grandchildren. (A.R.S. § 14-2103(1).)

### If you have no spouse and no children

Your estate passes to your parents. If both parents have passed, it goes to your siblings, then nieces and nephews, then grandparents, then aunts, uncles, and cousins — in that order, always by representation. (A.R.S. § 14-2103(2)–(4).)

### If you have no surviving relatives at all

Your entire estate goes to the State of Arizona. This is called "escheat," and while it's rare, it happens. (A.R.S. § 14-2105.)

## The Blended Family Problem

Arizona's intestacy rules create the most surprising outcomes for blended families — and blended families are increasingly common.

Consider a married couple. He has two children from a prior marriage. She has none. They've been together fifteen years and built a life together — a home, retirement accounts, savings. He passes away without a plan.

Under Arizona law, his wife does not inherit his half of the community property. His two adult children do. She keeps her own half of the community property, and she receives half of any separate property he owned. But the home they bought together during the marriage? She now co-owns it with his children. The joint savings account? Same.

This isn't a hypothetical edge case. It's the default rule for every blended family in Arizona that doesn't have an estate plan.

## Who Gets Left Out Entirely

Intestacy laws only recognize certain legal relationships. If someone matters to you but doesn't fit into the statutory hierarchy, they get nothing. Period.

People who receive no inheritance under Arizona intestacy law include stepchildren (unless you legally adopted them), unmarried domestic partners, close friends, charities or churches, foster children, and in-laws. It doesn't matter how long you've been together, how close the relationship was, or what promises were made. Without a will or trust, these people are invisible to the system.

On the other hand, people who do inherit under intestacy include legally adopted children (treated the same as biological children), children born outside of marriage (as long as parentage is established), half-siblings (treated the same as full siblings under A.R.S. § 14-2107), and children conceived before death but born after (as long as they survive at least 120 hours, per A.R.S. § 14-2108).

## A Few More Rules You Should Know

**The 120-hour rule.** To inherit under intestacy, a person must survive the deceased by at least 120 hours — five full days. If your brother is in the same car accident and dies three days after you, his estate doesn't receive your assets. They pass to the next person in line. (A.R.S. § 14-2104.)

**This only affects probate assets.** Intestacy rules apply only to assets owned solely in the deceased person's name with no beneficiary designation or automatic transfer. Anything in a living trust, jointly-titled accounts with rights of survivorship, life insurance, retirement accounts with named beneficiaries, and payable-on-death or transfer-on-death accounts all pass outside of probate — and outside of these rules entirely.

**Intestacy still means probate.** When there is no will or trust, the estate typically goes through probate — the court-supervised process of inventorying assets, paying debts, and distributing what's left. In Arizona, informal probate is straightforward, but it still takes six to twelve months, costs money, and becomes part of the public record.

## The Real Cost of No Plan

The financial cost of dying without a plan is real — probate fees, attorney fees, potential disputes among heirs. But the bigger cost is the one families feel most: confusion, conflict, and the knowledge that this isn't what their loved one would have wanted.

A will lets you choose who gets what and who's in charge of making it happen. A revocable living trust goes further — it keeps your family out of probate court entirely, protects your privacy, and provides for you during incapacity. Both are straightforward to create. Both are far less expensive than the alternative.

If you have a spouse, children, a home, or assets you care about passing to the right people, the question isn't whether you can afford to plan. It's whether your family can afford for you not to.
